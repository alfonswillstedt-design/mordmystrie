## The reveal — read aloud

*Read the verdict cards out loud, one at a time, before you begin.*

His name was Julian Reyes and he was a doctor, and that is why he was able to do it.

Fourteen months ago he wrote Iris Thornmere a prescription for diazepam and kept it off her record. She wasn't sleeping. She asked him at a dinner and said please, and he said yes, because it was easier than saying no and because he had done small favours like it before for people he liked.

He wrote nothing down. He told her not to mention it.

The night she died she had taken enough to be unsteady. She walked down to the lake the way she had every summer of her life, and she did not come back up.

Julian signed the death certificate himself. He left the toxicology section blank. He wrote *accident*, and he wrote it to protect his licence, and for fourteen months he believed that was the end of it.

Then Ambrose found the diary.

The last entry is dated three days before she died. *He said it would help me sleep. He said not to tell anyone he had given it to me.*

Ambrose had also requested her medical file. He had seen that two pages were missing. In the margin he had written: *diazepam — not in the record. Ask J.*

He invited all of you here to ask him in front of witnesses.

Some time before half past nine, Julian pushed a note under the study door. At ten to ten Ambrose walked down to the boathouse alone, in the rain, because someone had told him it concerned his sister's death. He lifted the lantern off its nail inside the door and lit it, and he stood there waiting, with Iris's diary lying open on the desk he had just left.

He was right. It did concern her death.

Julian had not planned the bollard. He had planned to talk. When talking failed he picked up what was nearest and struck once, downward and from the left, and then he stood in the rain and understood what he had done.

Then he did three things, and every one of them is on the table in front of you.

He went through Ambrose's pockets looking for his own note. He did not find it. It had gone through a gap in the boathouse floorboards and it was lying under his feet the whole time he searched.

He tore the hands off the smashed watch, so that nobody in this house could read a time from it except him.

And he left the lantern burning, because putting it out meant going back inside, and he had already started walking away.

And an hour later, standing over the body at the water's edge, he was the one who examined it. He told you the time of death was around half past ten — the moment the séance broke up, when everyone was moving about the house and nobody could account for anybody.

That is what caught him.

The assessment puts death between ten to ten and ten past. Julian put it twenty minutes beyond the latest it could have been and forty minutes beyond the earliest. No doctor is that far out, and the man who examined the body in the morning says so on the card. He said it to move the killing into the one part of the evening when nobody had an alibi at all.

Nadia's ninth frame was taken at four minutes past ten and shows the light burning in the boathouse window. Julian said he was in the library. The library window looks straight down at the water — forty metres, clear line of sight.

He saw it. Or he was never there.

As for the rest of you:

Callum burned his letters to Iris in the guest cottage and could not make himself say so out loud. Sabine was searching the study for the new will — the two creaks you heard during the séance were her going in and coming out again. Theo has been selling this house's antiques for over a year and thought you would hang him for it. Rowena knew the house was being sold and that she would lose her home. Gideon entered Iris into consecrated ground when he should not have. Marguerite has built a career on a legend she knows she invented.

Only one of you came into this house with nothing to hide, and it did not protect her for a moment. Nadia was accused as readily as anyone here, because honesty looks exactly like everything else in the dark.

So: one of you is a murderer, and almost every one of the rest of you spent tonight lying about something else.

And the lake never took a Thornmere. People did that, every time, for two hundred years.

## How you could have known

*Read this after the reveal. Take your time — this is the best part of the evening for anyone who guessed wrong.*

### Step one — everyone had a motive

Round one was designed to give you nothing. Every person at this table wanted Ambrose Thornmere inconvenienced, silenced, or dead. That was the point. Motive eliminates nobody here.

### Step two — the séance cleared two people

Gideon and Marguerite sat at that table from half past nine to half past ten and neither of them left it once (**C8**). They were seen there at the start, at the interruption, at the resumption, and at the end, and Rowena saw them both when she brought coffee in at five to ten and again at ten past. Neither of them could have been at the water. If you spent time on either of them, that time was wasted.

### Step three — the physical evidence cleared four more

**Callum** — the ashes in the cottage fireplace (**C15**) put him twenty metres from the body during the murder window, doing something he would rather have died than admit. He is the closest person in this story to the place where Ambrose died, and that is exactly why he had to be placed at the grate and not near it. He is. Frame seven of Nadia's camera, at one minute past ten, shows the cottage window lit and a figure standing at the fireplace (**C11**). Rowena saw the chimney smoking just before ten and still smoking thickly at ten past (**C16**), and letters burned one at a time have to be fed — a fire left alone dies down. Start of the window, middle of it, end of it. He looked guilty because he was hiding grief, not a killing.

**Sabine** — the study window is screwed shut from the inside and the only door creaks audibly into the drawing room below (**C20**). Marguerite logged that creak at ten to ten and a second one at twelve minutes past, and called them a presence arriving and withdrawing (**C14**). The murder window opens at ten to ten and closes at ten past. Sabine was behind that door from the minute it opened until after it shut. She lied about her location. She did not lie about the murder.

**Theo** — the dealer's receipt list (**C19**) explains what he was doing and why he would not say it. Frames four and twelve, at four minutes to ten and nine minutes past, both show the tool shed door open with a lamp lit and a figure at the bench (**C11**), and Rowena saw him cross the yard between the two, just after five past. The longest stretch in which nobody and nothing has him is nine minutes. The waterline is a walk of several minutes each way in the dark and the wet, and something happened at the end of it. Nine minutes does not cover the journey, let alone the killing.

**Rowena** — she carried coffee into the drawing room at five to ten and again at ten past, in front of the séance table and in front of two witnesses who never moved (**C8**), and Theo saw her through the laundry window just after five past. Five to, five past, ten past. That is the whole of the window in three pieces, and the wash she loaded at ten to ten ran on to prove she was in the laundry when it started (**C16**).

### Step four — two remained

Julian and Nadia. Both alone. Neither able to prove anything.

At this stage the evidence does not favour either. If you were choosing between them on instinct, you had a coin flip.

### Step five — the three chains that separate them

**The light and the window.** The boathouse was dark when Callum went down to the cottage, and he watched it come alight before ten. Nadia's ninth frame, timed at four minutes past ten, shows it still burning (**C11**). It was still burning when you found the body at twenty to eleven. The lantern hangs on a nail inside that door and does not light itself — Rowena will tell you so, and she is right.

That is the better part of an hour of a lit window at the water's edge, on a night when nobody had any business being down there.

The floor plan puts the library in the south-east corner with a window facing straight down at it, forty metres, unobstructed (**C13**). Julian claimed he sat in that room from twenty to ten until twenty-five past (**C12**).

If somebody at your table defends him on the grounds that a lit room turns a window into a mirror at night — that is a fair objection, and it does not save him. The point is not only that he failed to notice it at the time. It is that an hour later he stood at the waterline with that lantern burning over his shoulder, examined the body, gave an opinion on the time of death in front of all of you, and never once mentioned that he had spent the evening in the one room in this house that looks directly at it.

Nadia gains nothing from the photograph. It incriminates her as easily as it does anyone.

**The figure on the slope.** Nadia's private note puts somebody walking up from the water at about ten past ten. She assumed it was Theo, because Theo was the one person who might plausibly be out in that weather — and her own twelfth frame, taken one minute earlier, shows Theo at the tool shed bench with the lamp lit (**C11**).

So somebody who was not Theo came up from the water at the closing minute of the murder window. At ten past ten every other person in this house is held by something: the séance table, the coffee tray, the creak of the study door, the lit cottage window, the tool shed lamp.

Everyone except Julian.

This chain asks you to believe Nadia, and Nadia is the other person without an alibi. That is precisely why it is not the only chain.

**The time of death.** The assessment puts death between ten to ten and ten past, from an examination carried out at ten to six the following morning, the better part of eight hours after the fact, which is the interval at which temperature and rigor are at their most readable (**C17**). Julian, examining the body at ten to eleven at night, in the rain, put it at around half past ten — and that opinion is recorded in his own statement (**C12**).

That is twenty minutes beyond the latest the assessment allows and forty minutes beyond the earliest.

He will defend it, and he will defend it well: he had his hands on the body and the other doctor did not. It is a good line and it is precisely the wrong way round. An estimate made inside the first hour of death, in darkness and heavy rain, is the least reliable estimate obtainable — and the examining doctor says so on the card, before Julian ever opens his mouth.

It is also the single most useful lie available to him. Half past ten is the moment the séance broke up and the house filled with people moving between rooms. Had it been true, not one person here would have had an alibi — including the two who actually did.

There is a second problem with it, and it needs no forensics at all. The note told Ambrose to come to the boathouse at ten to ten (**C10**). If Julian's estimate were right, Ambrose stood at the water's edge in heavy rain for forty minutes waiting for somebody. Nobody does that. He went down at ten to ten and he died there, close to the time he arrived.

He was well placed to make it stick, too. He was the first person to examine him and the only one qualified to say anything at all about it — and he had already made certain that the watch on Ambrose's wrist would never contradict him.

### Step six — the motive that was always there

The certificate with the blank toxicology section (**C3**). The medical file with two pages missing and *ask J* in the margin (**C21**). The diary entry naming a man who gave her something and told her to keep quiet (**C22**).

Ambrose was going to read that aloud at midnight.

### The three details people ask about afterwards

**The blow came from above and slightly from the left (C18).** That is what a right-handed strike from behind looks like when the man in front of you is turning away. It rules nobody in and nobody out — Julian is right-handed, and so are five other people at that table.

**The last telephone call (C9).** Twelve seconds to a firm of solicitors at twenty-two minutes to ten. It looks like Sabine, and it is meant to. It was Ambrose: the firm on the log is the firm whose name is on the headed paper of the draft will lying on his desk (**C5**). He was chasing it. He wanted the new will signed before the divorce completed and he never got the chance, which is why the draft is still unsigned.

**The empty pockets and the broken watch (C1).** Julian searched him for the note and did not find it, because it had already gone through the boathouse floorboards, where it stayed until somebody thought to lift a board (**C10**). The hands of the watch were torn from the movement deliberately, and by the only person who needed them gone. A watch stopped at five to ten would have ended the evening before it began.

### What was designed to mislead you

The parish register (**C4**) and the row in the hall (**C6**). Both genuine, both irrelevant.

The will (**C5**) is a real motive and a strong one. It simply belongs to somebody who was standing behind a creaking door on the floor above at the time.
