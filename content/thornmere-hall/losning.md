## The reveal — read aloud

*Read the verdict cards out loud, one at a time, before you begin.*

His name was Julian Reyes and he was a doctor, and that is why he was able to do it.

Fourteen months ago he wrote Iris Thornmere a prescription for diazepam and kept it off her record. She wasn't sleeping. She asked him at a dinner and said please, and he said yes, because it was easier than saying no and because he had done small favours like it before for people he liked.

He wrote nothing down. He told her not to mention it.

The night she died she had taken enough to be unsteady. She walked down to the lake the way she had every summer of her life, and she did not come back up.

Julian signed the death certificate himself. He left the toxicology section blank. He wrote *accident*, and he wrote it to protect his licence, and for fourteen months he believed that was the end of it.

Then Ambrose found the diary.

The last entry is dated three days before she died. *He said it would help me sleep. He said not to tell anyone he had given it to me.*

Ambrose had also requested her medical file. He had seen that two pages were missing. In the margin he had written: *diazepam — not in the record. Ask J.*

He invited all of you here to ask him in front of witnesses.

Some time before half past nine, Julian pushed a note under the study door. At ten to ten Ambrose walked down to the boathouse alone, in the rain, because someone had told him it concerned his sister's death.

He was right. It did.

Julian had not planned the bollard. He had planned to talk. When talking failed he picked up what was nearest and struck once, downward and from the left, and then he stood in the rain and understood what he had done.

He left the lantern burning. He walked back to the house.

And an hour later, standing over the body at the water's edge, he was the one who examined it. He told you the time of death was around half past ten — the moment the séance broke up, when everyone was moving about the house and nobody could account for anybody.

That is what caught him.

The assessment puts death between ten to ten and a quarter past. Julian put it a quarter of an hour beyond that, and forty minutes past the earliest it could have been. No doctor is that far out on a body that has lain in ten-degree air. He said it to move the killing into the one part of the evening when nobody had an alibi at all.

Nadia's ninth frame was taken at four minutes past ten and shows the light burning in the boathouse window. Julian said he was in the library. The library window looks straight down at the water — forty metres, clear line of sight.

He saw it. Or he was never there.

As for the rest of you:

Callum burned his letters to Iris in the guest cottage and could not make himself say so out loud. Sabine was searching the study for the new will — the two creaks you heard during the séance were her going in and coming out again. Theo has been selling this house's antiques for over a year and thought you would hang him for it. Rowena knew the house was being sold and that she would lose her home. Gideon entered Iris into consecrated ground when he should not have. Marguerite has built a career on a legend she knows she invented.

Only one of you came into this house with nothing to hide, and it did not protect her for a moment. Nadia was accused as readily as anyone here, because honesty looks exactly like everything else in the dark.

So: one of you is a murderer, and almost every one of the rest of you spent tonight lying about something else.

And the lake never took a Thornmere. People did that, every time, for two hundred years.

## How you could have known

*Read this after the reveal. Take your time — this is the best part of the evening for anyone who guessed wrong.*

### Step one — everyone had a motive

Round one was designed to give you nothing. Every person at this table wanted Ambrose Thornmere inconvenienced, silenced, or dead. That was the point. Motive eliminates nobody here.

### Step two — the séance cleared two people

Gideon and Marguerite sat at that table from half past nine to half past ten and neither of them left it once (**C8**). They were seen there at the start, at the interruption, at the resumption, and at the end, and Rowena saw them both when she brought coffee in at five to ten and again at ten past. Neither of them could have been at the water. If you spent time on either of them, that time was wasted.

### Step three — the physical evidence cleared four more

**Callum** — the ashes in the cottage fireplace (**C15**) put him twenty metres from the body during the murder window, doing something he would rather have died than admit. Rowena saw the cottage chimney smoking just before ten and still smoking thickly at ten past (**C16**). Letters burned one at a time have to be fed; a fire left alone dies down. Somebody was standing over that grate for the whole of the murder window. He looked guilty because he was hiding grief, not a killing.

**Sabine** — the study window is screwed shut from the inside and the only door creaks audibly into the drawing room below (**C20**). Marguerite logged that creak at ten to ten and a second one at twelve minutes past, and called them a presence arriving and withdrawing (**C14**). Sabine was in that room for the whole of the murder window. She lied about her location. She did not lie about the murder.

**Theo** — the dealer's receipt list (**C19**) explains what he was doing and why he would not say it. Frames four and twelve of Nadia's contact sheet, at four minutes to ten and nine minutes past, both show the tool shed door open with a lamp lit and a figure at the bench (**C11**). Rowena saw him cross the yard between the two. He was at that bench for the whole of it.

**Rowena** — the wash cycle ran from just before ten until twenty to eleven (**C16**), Theo saw her just after five past, and she carried coffee into the drawing room at five to ten and again at ten past, in front of the séance table (**C8**). She is accounted for at both ends of the window and in the middle of it.

### Step four — two remained

Julian and Nadia. Both alone. Neither able to prove anything.

At this stage the evidence does not favour either. If you were choosing between them on instinct, you had a coin flip.

### Step five — the two chains that separate them

**The light and the window.** Nadia's ninth frame, timed at four minutes past ten, shows a lit window in the boathouse (**C11**). The lantern hangs on a nail inside that door and is not lit unless somebody walks down and lights it — Rowena will tell you so, and Callum saw it moving while he was burning his letters in the cottage. Somebody was down at the water during the murder window, carrying a light.

The floor plan puts the library in the south-east corner with a window facing the water, forty metres, clear sight (**C13**). Julian claimed he sat there from twenty to ten until twenty-five past (**C12**). A man in that room either sees that light and mentions it, or he was never in that room.

Nadia gains nothing from the photograph. It incriminates her as easily as anyone.

**The time of death.** The forensic assessment puts death between ten to ten and a quarter past, with a narrow margin (**C17**). Julian, examining the body at ten to eleven, put it at around half past ten — and that opinion is recorded in his own statement (**C12**).

That is a quarter of an hour beyond the latest the assessment allows, and forty minutes beyond the earliest. It is not an error a physician makes on a body that has lain in ten-degree air for under an hour.

It is also the single most useful lie available to him. Half past ten is the moment the séance broke up and the house filled with people moving between rooms. Had it been true, not one person here would have had an alibi — including the two who actually did.

There is a second problem with it, and it needs no forensics at all. The note told Ambrose to come to the boathouse at ten to ten (**C10**). If Julian's estimate were right, Ambrose stood at the water's edge in heavy rain for forty minutes waiting for somebody. Nobody does that. He went down at ten to ten and he died there, close to the time he arrived.

He was well placed to make it stick, too. He was the first person to examine him and the only one qualified to say anything at all about it. Had the watch on Ambrose's wrist survived the fall with its hands intact, he would never have got the words out.

### Step six — the motive that was always there

The certificate with the blank toxicology section (**C3**). The medical file with two pages missing and *ask J* in the margin (**C21**). The diary entry naming a man who gave her something and told her to keep quiet (**C22**).

Ambrose was going to read that aloud at midnight.

### The two details people ask about afterwards

**The blow came from above and slightly from the left (C18).** That is what a right-handed strike from behind looks like when the man in front of you is turning away. It rules nobody in and nobody out — Julian is right-handed, and so are five other people at that table.

**The last telephone call (C9).** Twelve seconds to a firm of solicitors at twenty-two minutes to ten. Ambrose was chasing the new will. He wanted it signed before the divorce completed and he never got the chance, which is why the draft on the desk is still unsigned.

### What was designed to mislead you

The parish register (**C4**), the will (**C5**), and the row in the hall (**C6**). All genuine, all irrelevant.
